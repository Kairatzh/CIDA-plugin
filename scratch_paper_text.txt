Abstract
Explicit reasoning methods such as chain-of-thought (CoT) improve multi-step performance,
but they cost generated tokens, add latency, and expose an intermediate trace that must be
generated, stored, and — in production systems — often hidden or discarded. We ask a
narrower question than “can models reason better”: can deliberation among specialized agents
take place inside a model’s latent space rather than in the space of generated tokens, while
still producing a usable, calibrated signal for downstream decoding or classification? We
present CIDA (Cognitive Interactive Deliberation Agents), a modular plug-in that attaches to a
frozen backbone without modifying its weights. A shared representation is projected into four
role-specialized latent states (Prosecutor, Defender, Skeptic, Integrator); the agents exchange
learned messages over T rounds through a disagreement-biased attention channel, and a
reliability-weighted product-of-experts (PoE) produces a consensus belief. We treat the four
architectural components — perspective projection, contrastive communication, reliability-
weighted consensus, and latent planning — as design principles rather than mechanisms we
have proven necessary or sufficient, and we report a theoretical property of the consensus
operator that holds regardless of whether the empirical gains we observe are attributable
to it. As a feasibility study under heavily constrained compute (a single Google Colab T4
or A100 instance, no dedicated cluster), we test two settings. On a 50k-image CheXpert
subset with a frozen DenseNet-121 backbone, CIDA adds 0.4M parameters (a 6% increase
over the 7.0M-parameter encoder head) and reaches mean AUC 0.939† and ECE 0.050 in
a single training run with a single seed. On language tasks, attaching CIDA to Qwen1.5-
7B-Chat raises GSM8K accuracy from 8.0% to 42.0% and BIG-Bench Hard from 4.4% to
44.0% relative to the same frozen backbone decoded greedily without CoT (N=50 samples
per task), with regressions on ARC-Challenge and HumanEval that we discuss at length in
Section 6 and Section 7. A preliminary efficiency profile shows CIDA generating roughly
a third of the tokens of CoT decoding at correspondingly lower accuracy. We are explicit
throughout this paper about what has not been established: the reasoning gains have not been
separated from the adapter’s added parameter capacity via a LoRA or soft-prompt control of
equal size; the medical-imaging result rests on a single seed with no confidence interval; and
several of the paper’s own source figures required correction before this draft (documented
in the author checklist, Appendix F). We view CIDA less as a finished method and more as
a scaffold for a specific, testable hypothesis about where deliberation can live in a neural
network, and we structure the paper — including an extended related-work comparison, full
architectural derivations, an algorithmic specification, and a reproducibility appendix — so

1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Over the last several years, the dominant lever for improving the reasoning ability of a fixed-size
language model at inference time has been to let it generate more text before committing to an answer.
Chain-of-thought prompting [1] showed that eliciting an intermediate, natural-language derivation
— even without any change to model weights — substantially improves accuracy on multi-step
arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning tasks. Tree of Thoughts [2] generalized this to an
explicit search over partial derivations, trading additional generated (and often discarded) tokens for
a higher probability of reaching a correct branch. Most recently, reinforcement-learned reasoning
models [3] have shown that this generate-and-search behavior can itself be learned end-to-end,
producing models that spontaneously emit very long chains of intermediate text before an answer.
These methods share a structural property that this paper takes as its starting point: the computation
that constitutes “thinking” is externalized into the same channel used for the final answer — the
sequence of output tokens. This has three consequences that are individually well known but rarely
discussed together as a single design choice. First, it is expensive: generating k tokens of scratch
reasoning costs the same marginal compute, per token, as generating k tokens of the final answer, even
though the scratch tokens are frequently thrown away. Second, it is slow in a way that compounds:
autoregressive decoding is inherently sequential, so a longer reasoning trace directly increases wall-
clock latency, which matters disproportionately in interactive or high-throughput deployment settings.
Third, it is exposed: the reasoning trace is, by construction, a sequence of natural-language tokens
that a user, a logging system, or an adversarial prompt can read, quote, or manipulate. Production
systems frequently respond to this third property by hiding or summarizing the trace after the fact,
which does not remove the first two costs and arguably makes the method less inspectable rather than
more.
Multi-agent debate [4] pushes in a related but distinct direction: rather than having one model reason
longer, it has several instances of a model (or several different models) argue with each other in
natural language and aggregates their conclusions. This improves factuality on some tasks, but
multiplies the token cost of a single reasoning episode by the number of debating agents and the
number of debate rounds, compounding exactly the costs described above.
A separate line of work asks whether some of the benefit of explicit reasoning can be obtained
without paying its full token cost by moving part of the computation into continuous, non-token
space. Coconut [5] feeds a model’s own continuous hidden state back in as an input for subsequent
reasoning steps, rather than decoding it to a token first. Quiet-STaR [6] trains a model to produce
internal rationales that influence its predictions without being read as text. Pause tokens [7] give
a model extra forward-pass positions with no linguistic content, purely to allocate more compute
before an answer. Separately, prompt tuning [8] and prefix tuning [9] show that a small number of
learned continuous vectors, prepended to a frozen model’s input, can condition its behavior about
as effectively as an explicit natural-language prompt for many tasks — without themselves being
interpretable as text at all.
CIDA sits at the intersection of these two observations. If (a) some of what chain-of-thought buys
us is better modeled as allocating more computation before committing to an answer rather than as
literally reasoning in natural language, and if (b) continuous vectors can condition a frozen backbone
about as well as a discrete prompt can, then it is natural to ask whether the process of arriving at
that continuous vector can itself be structured as something resembling deliberation — multiple
partial, disagreeing views of the input that interact and are reconciled — rather than being a single
feed-forward projection. This is the hypothesis this paper investigates, not a hypothesis this paper
claims to have confirmed.

1.2
What this paper does and does not claim
We want to be unusually explicit, this early in the paper, about the shape of the evidence we present,
because the architecture (Section 3) is more fully specified than the empirical support behind any
individual design choice within it.
What we built. A plug-in module, CIDA, that can be attached to a frozen encoder (for classification)
or a frozen decoder-only language model (for generation) without modifying the backbone’s weights.
The module instantiates four role-specialized latent agents that exchange messages over T rounds
and produce a single consensus belief via a reliability-weighted product-of-experts combination.
What we measured. Two feasibility studies under constrained compute: an image-classification
setting (CheXpert chest X-ray multi-label classification with a frozen DenseNet-121 encoder) and
a language-reasoning setting (five benchmarks with a frozen Qwen backbone at several parameter
scales). We also ran a component-ablation study on the imaging setting and a lightweight efficiency
profile comparing token count, latency, and throughput against chain-of-thought decoding.
What we did not do. We did not run more than one random seed for the imaging experiment. We
did not evaluate on the full CheXpert validation set for the language-adjacent claims (our language
evaluations use N=30–50 examples per benchmark, chosen to fit within the compute and time
available on free or low-tier cloud GPU instances). We did not construct a LoRA or soft-prompt
baseline with the same number of trainable parameters as the CIDA adapter, which means the
reasoning-benchmark gains reported in Section 6 cannot currently be attributed to the deliberation
mechanism specifically, as opposed to simply having more task-adapted parameters attached to
the frozen backbone. We did not run a multi-seed or bootstrapped significance test for any of the
headline numbers. We did not obtain any form of external clinical validation for the medical-imaging
component, and CIDA is not, and is not intended to be, a clinical decision tool in its current form.
We return to this list, with more detail on each point, in Section 8, and we use it to motivate the
specific future experiments proposed in Section 9.
1.3
Contributions
Concretely, this paper contributes:
1. An architecture (Section 3) that decomposes latent deliberation into four separable compo-
nents — perspective projection, contrastive communication, reliability-weighted consensus, and
latent planning — each individually ablatable, together with a full algorithmic specification
(Appendix B) and a derivation of the one theoretical property we can state precisely about the
consensus operator (Section 3.5, proof in Appendix C).
2. Two feasibility studies (Section 6) under realistic compute constraints (a single consumer-grade
or free-tier cloud GPU), reported with explicit sample sizes, seeds, and — where computable —
confidence intervals, rather than as polished, cluster-scale benchmark numbers.
3. A component ablation (Section 6) isolating the individual contribution of each architectural
piece on the imaging task, and a discussion (Section 7) of which of the four components the
ablation does and does not support.
4. An honest accounting of what remains unresolved (Section 8), including a documented author
checklist (Appendix F) listing specific numerical and figure inconsistencies between our internal
logs and earlier drafts of this paper, corrected in the version presented here.
5. A reproducibility appendix (Appendices A–E) with full notation, pseudocode, proof details,
and the hyperparameters we can currently confirm, so that the specific, falsifiable claims in this
paper can be checked or extended by others with more compute.

Related Work
2.1
Calibration and uncertainty quantification
Modern neural networks trained with cross-entropy loss tend to be overconfident: their predicted
probabilities systematically overstate the true frequency of correctness, especially as model capacity
grows [10]. The standard post-hoc fix, temperature scaling, rescales the logits of an already-trained
network using a single scalar fit on a held-out set; it is cheap, requires no architectural change,
and — as we confirm in Section 6 — is a strong baseline that CIDA does not beat on our imaging
task. Methods that intervene during training rather than after it include focal loss [11], which down-
weights well-classified examples to discourage overconfidence directly in the training objective, and
deep ensembles [12], which train several independently initialized networks and treat disagreement
between them as an uncertainty signal. Evidential deep learning [13] takes a different approach again,
having the network output the parameters of a Dirichlet distribution over class probabilities rather
than a point estimate, so that the width of that distribution serves as a built-in uncertainty measure.
CIDA’s reliability-weighted consensus (Section 3.5) is closest in spirit to deep ensembles: uncertainty
is read off from disagreement between several sub-models (here, agents) rather than being predicted
directly by a single network head. The important architectural difference is that CIDA’s four agents
are not independently trained, share a single upstream representation h, and explicitly communicate
before their beliefs are combined — so the classical justification for using ensemble disagreement
as an uncertainty proxy, which typically assumes some degree of independence between ensemble
members, does not obviously transfer. We discuss this tension directly in Section 3.5 and Appendix C:
the one property we can prove about CIDA’s consensus operator holds without an independence
assumption, but it is a weaker property than “the agents behave like an ensemble,” and we are careful
not to claim the stronger one.
2.2
Explicit test-time reasoning
Chain-of-thought prompting [1] established that asking a sufficiently large language model to produce
intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer improves performance on tasks that require
multi-step inference, without any change to model weights. Tree of Thoughts [2] treats the space
of possible partial derivations as a search tree, allowing the model to backtrack from unpromising
branches — at the cost of generating and evaluating many more candidate continuations than a single
greedy CoT trace. Most recently, reinforcement-learning-trained reasoning models [3] internalize a
search-like process end-to-end during training, producing models that autonomously decide how long
a reasoning trace to generate, sometimes extending to thousands of tokens for difficult problems. All
three approaches share the property discussed in Section 1: the deliberation process is materialized as
a sequence of tokens in the same vocabulary and the same autoregressive channel as the final answer.
2.3
Latent and implicit reasoning
A smaller but growing body of work asks whether the benefits of extended test-time computation can
be obtained without paying the full cost of generating and decoding natural-language tokens for the
intermediate steps. Coconut [5] replaces the standard chain-of-thought decoding loop with one in
which the model’s last hidden state is fed back in directly as the next input embedding, skipping the
detokenize/retokenize round trip entirely for intermediate steps. Quiet-STaR [6] trains a model to
generate parallel internal rationales at every token position and to learn, via reinforcement signal,
6


=== PAGE 7 ===
which of them actually help predict the next token — the rationales influence behavior without
necessarily being decoded or read as text. Pause tokens [7] take an even more minimal approach:
they insert placeholder positions with no linguistic content into the input, purely so the model has
extra forward-pass computation available before it must commit to a prediction, and show that this
alone can help on some tasks.
Separately, and prior to this line of “implicit reasoning” work, prompt tuning [8] and prefix tuning [9]
demonstrated that a small number of learned continuous vectors — not corresponding to any token in
the model’s vocabulary — prepended to a frozen model’s input sequence can condition its behavior
on a downstream task about as effectively as a hand-written natural-language prompt, for many tasks
and model scales. CIDA’s “plan tokens” (Section 3.6) are architecturally closest to this second family:
they are exactly P learned continuous vectors prepended to the decoder’s input. What CIDA adds
relative to plain prompt or prefix tuning is that the vectors are not a fixed, input-independent parameter,
nor a single feed-forward projection of the input; they are the output of a structured, multi-step,
multi-agent process applied to the input’s own representation. Whether this added structure earns its
keep relative to a soft prompt of equal parameter count is, as stated repeatedly in this paper, an open
question we have not yet tested (Section 8).
We should also situate CIDA relative to low-rank adaptation [14], which is not a latent-reasoning
method at all but is the most common way to attach a small number of trainable parameters to a
frozen backbone, and is therefore the natural capacity-matched control we are missing: a LoRA
adapter with a parameter count equal to CIDA’s, trained on the same data, would tell us how much of
CIDA’s benefit is attributable to having extra task-specific parameters at all, independent of whether
those parameters are organized as “agents” that “deliberate.”
2.4
Multi-agent debate
Du et al. [4] show that having several instances of a language model generate answers, read each
other’s natural-language answers, and revise their own in light of that exchange — repeated over
several rounds — improves factual accuracy and reasoning correctness relative to a single model
instance answering once. This is architecturally the closest prior work to CIDA in one specific sense:
it also involves several sub-agents whose individual judgments are reconciled into a final answer
through iterated exchange. The channel of exchange is the entire difference. In text-based debate,
each agent reads and writes full natural-language responses, so a debate with M agents over R rounds
costs on the order of M ×R full generations, and the entire exchange is visible and auditable, but also
expensive and slow, compounding the same costs discussed in Section 1 for a single model’s chain of
thought. CIDA replaces this text channel with learned continuous messages exchanged inside a single
forward pass: the four agents never generate text at all during deliberation, and the entire T-round
exchange happens within the computational cost of one forward pass through the plug-in module,
not M ×R additional generations. This buys efficiency at a real cost: unlike a text transcript, CIDA’s
intermediate messages are not natively human-readable, and we have not yet built or evaluated any
interpretability tooling for inspecting what the agents “say” to each other (see Section 9, “Research
directions”).
2.5
Medical imaging and calibrated diagnosis support
CheXpert [15] and the earlier CheXNet [16] established large-scale chest radiograph classification
as a standard benchmark for evaluating both the raw discriminative accuracy and, increasingly, the
calibration quality of vision models intended to eventually support (not replace) a radiologist’s
judgment. Uncertainty estimation specifically for diagnostic imaging has been studied both in the
context of COVID-19 chest imaging [17] and more generally across disease categories [18], with both
lines of work emphasizing that a model’s raw softmax confidence is a poor proxy for how often it is
actually correct, motivating exactly the kind of explicit calibration measurement (expected calibration
error, Section 5) that we report for CIDA in Section 6.

2.6
Qualitative comparison
Table 1 summarizes the discussion above along four axes that matter for deployment: whether the
method requires generating additional natural-language tokens, whether the intermediate computation
is human-inspectable without extra tooling, whether the method requires any training or fine-tuning
at all (as opposed to being usable zero-shot on a frozen model), and whether multiple distinct
“perspectives” or sub-agents are used. This table is a qualitative characterization based on the cited
papers’ own descriptions of their methods, not a benchmark run by us; we did not re-implement
or re-evaluate any of these baselines ourselves, and the table should be read as a design-space map
rather than a leaderboard. An expanded version with more axes and short justifying notes is given in
Appendix D.
The table makes CIDA’s specific position legible: it is the only method in this list that is simultaneously
token-free at inference time and explicitly multi-perspective. That combination is the entire hypothesis
under test in this paper — and, as the “human-readable” column makes visible, it is purchased at the
same interpretability cost paid by every other latent-only method in the table.
3
The CIDA Architecture
This section gives the complete architecture, one component at a time. Each subsection states the
equation, then explains why that particular form was chosen and what alternative we considered
and did not pursue. A summary of all notation used in this section is collected in Appendix A for
reference, and the full forward pass is given as pseudocode in Appendix B.
At a high level, a frozen backbone (a convolutional encoder for the imaging setting, or a decoder-only
transformer for the language setting) produces a single context vector h ∈Rdin summarizing the
current input. CIDA never modifies the backbone that produces h; it only consumes it. Starting from
h, CIDA runs T rounds of deliberation among M=4 agents and returns a single consensus belief p
(imaging setting) or a sequence of P plan-token embeddings (language setting) that is handed back
to the frozen backbone. Figure 1 shows the imaging configuration; Figure 2 shows the language
configuration.
3.1
Why four agents, and why these four roles
The choice of M=4 agents, and the specific roles Prosecutor, Defender, Skeptic, and Integrator,
is a design choice inspired loosely by adversarial and deliberative structures in human decision-
making (courtroom argument, red-team/blue-team analysis, editorial review) rather than a value
derived from any optimization or search over M. We have not run an ablation over M ∈{2,3,4,5,6};
the ablation we do report (Section 6) removes components of the architecture (communication,
consensus weighting, and so on) while holding M=4 fixed throughout. The specific naming of the
four roles (Prosecutor, Defender, Skeptic, Integrator) does not correspond to an explicit, differently-

preliminary by design (single seed, small N), we wanted at least one claim that does not depend on
our own measurements at all, and we would rather state precisely what that claim does and does not
imply than let it be read as stronger support for the architecture than it provides.
3.6
Latent Planning for Language Models
For a language backbone, the final hidden state produced by the frozen encoder is processed by the
full CIDA module exactly as described above, but instead of terminating in a single scalar consensus
belief p (as in the imaging setting), the converged agent states are projected into P=4–8 virtual
plan-token embeddings, which are prepended to the frozen decoder’s input sequence (Figure 2). The
decoder then generates the answer autoregressively, exactly as it would with any other input, with
no architectural awareness that its first P input positions were produced by a deliberation process
rather than being ordinary token embeddings. No reasoning text is produced during the deliberation
phase itself — deliberation is entirely latent — but the resulting sequence the decoder consumes is P
tokens longer than it would otherwise be, and those P extra positions do consume compute during the
decoder’s forward pass, exactly as any other input token would.
We chose P ∈[4,8] (rather than a single fixed value) because we observed, informally and without
a systematic sweep, that very small P (1–2) seemed to bottleneck the amount of information the
consensus could carry into the decoder, while larger P (beyond roughly 8–10) began to erode the
token and latency advantage that motivates using latent planning in the first place (Section 6.4). We
report this as an informal observation, not a tuned or validated hyperparameter choice; Appendix E
lists the exact value of P used for each reported result where we can confirm it from our logs, and
marks it as unconfirmed where we cannot.
3.7
Computational cost
Relative to running the frozen backbone alone, CIDA adds: (i) M per-agent projections (Equation 1),
each roughly the cost of one linear layer from din to dagent; (ii) T rounds of message computation
and attention among M agents, each round costing O(M2) pairwise attention terms — negligible
for M=4 — plus M applications of the state-update block; and (iii), in the language setting only,
one additional projection from the converged agent states to P plan-token embeddings. None of
this touches the frozen backbone’s own forward pass, so the marginal cost of CIDA is entirely a
function of (M,T,dagent) and, for the language setting, P, and is independent of the backbone’s
own size beyond the cost of reading h once per input. In parameter-count terms this is small by
construction: 0.4M added parameters against a 7.0M-parameter DenseNet-121 head in the imaging
setting (Section 6), and adapters ranging from under 1M to 160M parameters across the four language-
model configurations in Table 2. We do not currently report a wall-clock breakdown of how much of
CIDA’s own forward pass (as opposed to the frozen backbone’s) contributes to the latency numbers
in Table 6; this is listed as an open item in Section 8.

4
Implementation Details
This section documents engineering and software choices that do not appear in the equations of
Section 3 but that affect whether another group can reproduce, or meaningfully compare against, our
results. We separate this from the architecture section deliberately: the architecture is a set of design
claims; this section is a set of factual claims about what we actually ran, to the extent our logs support
them.
4.1
Plug-in design and the frozen-backbone contract
CIDA is implemented as a wrapper module that consumes a single context vector h and returns
either a calibrated belief p (imaging) or a sequence of plan-token embeddings (language); it has no
dependency on the internal architecture of whatever produced h, beyond needing to know din. In
practice this means the same CIDA implementation was attached, with no architectural change to the
plug-in itself, to a convolutional encoder (DenseNet-121) in the imaging setting and to four different
sizes of a decoder-only transformer (Qwen1.5-1.8B, Qwen1.5-7B-Chat, Qwen-7B, Qwen2.5-1.5B)
13


=== PAGE 14 ===
in the language setting. The backbone’s own parameters are frozen throughout training in every
configuration reported in this paper: gradients flow into the CIDA module and, in the language setting,
into the plan-token projection, but never into the backbone’s own weights. This is both a scientific
choice (it isolates what the added module can and cannot contribute on top of a fixed, unmodified
base model) and a practical one (it is the only way the language-model experiments were feasible at
all on the hardware described in Section 5, since backpropagating through a 7B-parameter model’s
own weights was outside our compute budget).
4.2
Software and release artifacts
The reference implementation is released as a pip-installable package (cida-plugin) together
with the training and evaluation code on GitHub (github.com/Kairatzh/CIDA-plugin), and the
trained language-model adapter is released as kirtzh/Kairos-7B-CIDA-v1.5. We rely on the
standard open-source deep learning stack (PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, and the timm library
for the DenseNet-121 backbone), acknowledged at the end of this paper. We do not currently provide
a hosted inference endpoint or a Docker image; running the released code requires a local or cloud
GPU environment compatible with the frozen backbones described above.
4.3
Training procedure
For the imaging setting, the CIDA module and its consensus head are trained end-to-end against the
standard multi-label binary cross-entropy loss used for CheXpert, summed across the 14 pathology
labels, plus the orthogonality regularizer of Equation 2 with a fixed scalar weight. Training uses
AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule, batch size 32, for 15 epochs on a single A100 (40GB)
GPU via Google Colab Pro+. For the language setting, the adapter (CIDA module plus plan-token
projector) is trained against the task’s own next-token prediction loss on the target answer span, with
the frozen backbone providing gradients only up to, and not through, its own weights. Table 2 lists,
per language-model configuration, the training-set size and adapter parameter count we can currently
confirm from our logs; where a cell in that table is marked with a dagger, the exact figure is pending
reconciliation against the raw logs (see Appendix F), and readers should treat those specific numbers
as approximate pending a corrected release.
4.4
Compute budget and its consequences
Every experiment in this paper was run on a single free-tier or Pro+-tier Google Colab instance
(an NVIDIA T4 or A100 GPU), with no access to a dedicated multi-GPU cluster, no ability to
run large hyperparameter sweeps, and hard session-length limits that directly constrain how large
a training set, how many epochs, and how many evaluation samples we could use for any single
reported number. This is stated plainly, and repeatedly, throughout this paper (rather than only in this
subsection) because it is the single biggest factor determining the kind of evidence this paper can
offer: we can report a single well-instrumented run per configuration, with sample sizes chosen to
complete within a Colab session, but we cannot currently report the multi-seed, large-N, statistically
powered comparisons that would be standard for a paper making a confident architectural claim.
Every subsequent numerical result in this paper should be read against this constraint.
4.5
Reproducibility caveats specific to this draft
In preparing this extended edition, we cross-checked the figures and tables in an earlier internal

5
Experimental Protocol
All experiments were run under the constrained-compute conditions described in Section 4.4 (Google
Colab, NVIDIA T4 or A100). They are feasibility studies; we report sizes, seeds, and settings
explicitly throughout this section so that the level of evidence behind each number in Section 6 is
unambiguous.
5.1
Medical imaging protocol
We use CheXpert v1.0 [15], restricted to a 50,000-image subset drawn with a fixed random seed (42)
for tractability under our compute budget, rather than the full ∼224,000-image training set. The
backbone is a DenseNet-121 [20] pretrained on ImageNet and then frozen; only the CIDA module
(Section 3) and the multi-label classification head are trained, for 15 epochs, batch size 32, AdamW
optimizer with a cosine learning-rate schedule, on a single A100 (40GB) GPU. CheXpert includes
an “uncertain” label option for each of its 14 pathology categories, reflecting genuine radiologist
uncertainty in the source annotations; following common practice on this dataset, we map uncertain
labels to positive (the “U-Ones” convention). This experiment uses a single training run with a single
random seed; we did not train multiple seeds and therefore cannot report a confidence interval or
variance estimate for the headline AUC and ECE numbers in Table 3, which is the single largest
statistical limitation of the imaging result (see Section 8).

5.3
Efficiency profiling protocol
The efficiency comparison in Table 6 and Figure 7 is a live profile of CIDA (v1.5, Qwen-7B backbone)
against the same backbone using standard chain-of-thought decoding, measured on the same hardware.
We flag an important asymmetry in how the two accuracy numbers in that table were obtained: the
CoT accuracy figure (62.5% on GSM8K) is a previously published, full-test-set result for Qwen-7B
with CoT prompting, whereas the CIDA accuracy figure (55.4%) is our own 50-sample estimate
under the protocol described above. The two numbers are therefore not a paired, identically-sampled
comparison, and Table 6’s caption states this explicitly; the token-count, latency, and throughput
comparisons in the same table, by contrast, were measured by us on the same hardware for both
systems and are a fair, paired comparison.
5.4
Ablation protocol
The component ablation (Table 4, Figure 6) removes one architectural component of CIDA at a
time from the full imaging configuration described above, retraining from scratch for each variant
under the same 50k-subset, 15-epoch, single-seed protocol, and reports the resulting AUC and ECE.
Because every variant, including the full model, uses a single seed, the ablation can indicate the
direction and rough relative magnitude of each component’s contribution, but — as we state again
in Section 7 — cannot statistically distinguish between two components whose single-seed effect
sizes are close to each other, since we have no estimate of the run-to-run variance any one of these
numbers would exhibit under a different seed.

F1 is comparatively low on Lung Lesion, Fracture, Enlarged Cardiomediastinum, and Pleural Other
(Figure 5). All four are among the rarer findings in the CheXpert label distribution, and we read
this pattern as most likely reflecting class imbalance in the 50k training subset rather than the model
confusing these findings with unrelated ones — but we have not run a dedicated confusion-matrix
analysis to confirm that the errors on these classes are false negatives driven by rarity, as opposed to
systematic confusion with a specific other class, and we list this as an open item in Section 8.
6.2
Ablation
Table 4 and Figure 6 report the component ablation described in Section 5. Removing inter-agent
communication (Section 3.3) entirely causes the single largest drop in both AUC and ECE among all
the variants tested, and replacing the reliability-weighted product-of-experts consensus (Section 3.5)
with simple, unweighted mean aggregation produces the second-largest drop. Removing role spe-
cialization (the orthogonality regularizer of Equation 2) and removing the adaptive disagreement
schedule of Equation 4 produce smaller, but still monotonic, degradations, and removing perspective
projection (Equation 1, replaced with a shared, untied projection) produces the smallest degradation
of the five variants relative to the full model — though still a clear gap relative to the frozen-DenseNet
baseline with no CIDA module at all.

Preliminary reasoning results

biasing the decoder toward a “derive step by step” mode that helps when derivation is the bottleneck
and actively hurts when precise recall or exact syntax is the bottleneck instead. We have not tested
this explanation directly — doing so would require, at minimum, inspecting whether the regression is
concentrated in specific HumanEval problem categories (e.g., problems requiring precise API usage
versus problems requiring algorithmic reasoning) or whether it is uniform across the benchmark.

should be attributed: the CIDA adapter used for these five benchmarks has tens of millions of
trainable parameters (Table 2), and we have not run a LoRA or soft-prompt control adapter

6.4
Efficiency profile
Table 6 and Figure 7 report the live efficiency comparison described in Section 5.3. CIDA generates
roughly a third of the tokens that chain-of-thought decoding does for the same task and backbone,
and reduces per-query latency by roughly half, while also showing lower accuracy on the (non-paired,
see Section 5.3) GSM8K comparison in the same table.

strictly beat CoT decoding on every axis, it beats it on token count, latency, and throughput while
trading away some accuracy. We compare only two operating points here (CIDA’s fixed P ∈[4,8]
plan tokens versus CoT’s typical generation length on this task) and do not currently have a full
accuracy–latency Pareto curve that would show, for instance, whether a larger P or more deliberation
rounds T could recover some of the accuracy gap at a smaller efficiency cost than falling back to full
CoT. We also note that CIDA’s shorter outputs may partly reflect properties of its training data (which

7
Analysis
This section steps back from individual numbers and asks what, in total, the results in Section 6 do
and do not support about the architectural hypothesis stated in Section 1.
7.1
Does inter-agent communication matter?
The ablation (Table 4) is the closest thing in this paper to direct evidence on this question, and the
answer it gives, carefully stated, is: removing communication produces the largest single degradation
among the five components tested, which is consistent with the exchange of learned messages between
agents carrying information that is useful to the downstream consensus, beyond what each agent’s
own local projection of h already contains. This is not the same as proof that the specific contrastive,
disagreement-seeking form of that communication (Equation 4) is what matters, as opposed to any
form of inter-agent information exchange; a control in which agents exchange messages via ordinary
similarity-seeking attention, rather than the disagreement-biased form we use, would be needed to
isolate that specific design choice, and we have not run it.
We would also like to inspect what the agents’ messages actually encode — do they, over the course
of training, come to specialize in a way that resembles the Prosecutor/Defender/Skeptic/Integrator
naming scheme, or do the roles remain largely interchangeable up to the fixed orthogonality regu-
larizer? This is exactly the question Figure 3 was originally meant to speak to, and we want to be
unambiguous about its actual status: Figure 3 is a schematic of the behavior the architecture is
designed to encourage, not a plot of measured per-agent belief trajectories from real model
inputs. We have not yet built the logging infrastructure to capture real per-round agent beliefs
during inference on held-out examples, and until that exists, any statement about what the four
agents actually converge toward — including the evocative Prosecutor/Defender/Skeptic/Integrator
framing itself — should be understood as a description of the training-time inductive bias we tried to
install, not as a confirmed empirical property of the trained model. An earlier internal draft of this
paper included two figures (belief trajectories and attention maps) that appeared to show exactly this
kind of real per-agent behavior; on inspection they showed clear artifacts of having been produced
by an unconstrained generative process rather than logged from the model, and we removed them
(Section 4.5, Appendix F) rather than let a fabricated-looking figure stand in for the real analysis that
has not yet been done.
7.2
Can deliberation help a smaller model?
CIDA v2 (Qwen1.5-1.8B backbone, trained on a 40k-example adversarial logic training set) scores
93.3% (28/30; 95% Wilson interval ≈79–98%) on a held-out set of adversarial negation problems
(Table 7), compared to 30.0% for CIDA v1 (the much larger Qwen1.5-7B-Chat backbone), which
was not trained on this task distribution.
The honest reading of Table 7 is narrower than “a small model with CIDA beats a large model,”
because the comparison is confounded by task-specific training: the v2 adapter was trained specifically
on this task distribution, and neither the base models nor CIDA v1 were. What the table actually shows
is that a small backbone with a task-trained adapter can outperform a much larger backbone without
21


=== PAGE 22 ===
one — which is a real and useful finding for practitioners deciding whether to fine-tune a small model
or rely on a larger untuned one for a narrow task, but it is a finding about the value of task-specific
training data, not, by itself, a finding about the value of the deliberation architecture relative to some
other equally task-trained adapter of similar size. Isolating the architecture’s contribution on this
specific task would again require the LoRA/soft-prompt control discussed throughout this paper.
7.3
Data efficiency
CIDA v3 (Qwen2.5-1.5B backbone, an adapter with fewer than 1M trainable parameters, trained on
18k curated ProofWriter and GSM8K examples in a single A100 session) reaches 79% exact match on
an out-of-distribution logic benchmark. This is, by a wide margin, the smallest adapter and smallest
training set of any configuration in this paper, and the result is encouraging as a data-point for the
claim that latent deliberation does not require large adapters to show some effect. It is nonetheless
a single small evaluation on a single benchmark, and we do not currently report the size of the
out-of-distribution evaluation set itself in a way we are confident is accurate (see Appendix F); we list
reporting that figure precisely, and expanding the evaluation set size, as a concrete near-term action
item in Section 9.
7.4
What the regressions might be telling us
The consistent regression on ARC-Challenge and HumanEval across the language-model results
(Table 5) is, in our reading, one of the more informative results in this paper precisely because it
is a negative result. If CIDA’s plan tokens were simply adding generic task-agnostic capacity to
the frozen backbone, we might expect either uniform improvement or roughly noise-level, non-
systematic change across benchmark categories. Instead, we see a clean split: benchmarks requiring
multi-step derivation improve substantially, while a knowledge-recall benchmark and a precise-syntax
code-generation benchmark both regress by a similar, non-trivial margin. This pattern is at least
suggestive that the plan tokens are doing something benchmark-category-specific rather than acting as
a generic capacity boost — which would itself be mild evidence against the “it’s just extra parameters”
explanation discussed in Section 6.3 — but we want to be careful not to over-read a five-benchmark,
N=50-per-benchmark pattern as a confirmed mechanism. Distinguishing “benchmark-category-
specific interference” from “generic capacity with benchmark-category-specific sensitivity” would
require exactly the LoRA-control experiment we do not yet have: if a LoRA adapter of equal size,
trained on the same data, showed the same category-specific pattern (gains on derivation tasks,
regressions on recall/syntax tasks), that would suggest the pattern is a property of the training data
rather than of latent deliberation specifically; if it showed a different pattern, that would be much
stronger evidence for the architecture itself mattering.
7.5
A distributed-systems analogy, offered cautiously
It may be useful, for readers familiar with distributed systems or classical ensemble methods, to
note an informal analogy between CIDA’s consensus mechanism and quorum-based agreement
protocols: each agent contributes a “vote” (its belief bT
i ) weighted by a measure of both its current
confidence and its historical reliability, and the final decision is a weighted aggregate rather than
a simple majority. We offer this only as an intuition pump, not as a formal connection — CIDA’s
agents are not fault-tolerant in the Byzantine sense (a single confidently wrong agent with high
historical reliability ρi can still dominate the PoE weighting in Equation 6), and Proposition 1 is a
statement about log-linear pooling in information theory, not a statement about consensus protocols in
distributed computing. We mention the analogy because it partly motivated the reliability-weighting
design in Section 3.5, and because we think it is a productive direction for future theoretical work
(Section 9) rather than because we have established any formal connection in this paper.

8
Limitations
We collect, in one place, every limitation raised piecemeal throughout the paper, organized by
category, so that a reader deciding how much weight to place on any given claim in this paper has a
single reference point.
8.1
Statistical limitations
• The medical-imaging result (Section 6.1) uses a single training run with a single random seed; we
have no estimate of run-to-run variance for the headline AUC or ECE, and cannot rule out that a
different seed would produce a meaningfully different number.
• The ablation (Section 6.2) likewise uses a single seed per variant; the ordering of the five archi-
tectural components by effect size should be read as indicative of direction, not as a statistically
validated ranking.
• The language-model reasoning evaluations (Section 6.3) use N=30–50 examples per benchmark,
chosen for compute tractability rather than statistical power; the resulting confidence intervals
(roughly ±14 percentage points at N=50) are wide relative to some of the smaller reported deltas
(e.g., the +8.0pp gain on MATH-500).
• No result in this paper has been validated with a bootstrap, permutation test, or any other
significance-testing procedure beyond the closed-form binomial/Wilson intervals reported for the
N-sample accuracy estimates.
8.2
Attribution limitations
• We have not run an equal-capacity LoRA or soft-prompt control for any of the language-model
reasoning results, which means the gains in Table 5 cannot currently be attributed to the delibera-
tion mechanism specifically, as opposed to added parameter count in general. This is, in our own
assessment, the single most consequential open gap in this paper (Sections 6.3, 7).
• Several external baselines in Table 3 (temperature scaling, deep ensembles, the base DenseNet-
121 figure itself) are drawn from public reports on similar, but not identical, configurations, rather
than re-run by us under matched conditions; differences in training subset, augmentation, or
optimizer schedule between our run and the reported baselines could each independently account
for part of the observed gap.
• The CIDA v2 small-model result (Table 7, Section 7) is confounded by task-specific training
data that the comparison baselines did not receive, and should not be read as an architecture-only
comparison.
8.3
Scope and coverage limitations
• Two tasks (chest radiograph multi-label classification and five language-reasoning benchmarks)
is a narrow empirical base for a general architectural claim; we have not tested CIDA on other
modalities, other medical-imaging datasets, or a broader span of language tasks beyond those in
Table 5.
• No external clinical validation of any kind has been performed for the medical-imaging component.
CIDA is not a clinical decision tool, has not been evaluated prospectively, and nothing in this
paper should be read as supporting its use in a clinical setting without substantial further validation
(see also Section 10).
• We have not evaluated CIDA’s robustness to distribution shift beyond the single out-of-distribution
logic evaluation in the data-efficiency result (Section 7), nor to adversarial inputs targeting the
deliberation mechanism itself.
8.4
Mechanistic and interpretability limitations
• We have not logged or inspected real per-agent belief trajectories or attention patterns from
actual model inputs; Figure 3 is a conceptual schematic of design intent, not measured behavior
(Section 7).
23


=== PAGE 24 ===
• We do not currently have any tooling for inspecting what information CIDA’s inter-agent messages
(Equation 3) actually carry, beyond the indirect evidence provided by the communication ablation.
• We have not compared the contrastive, disagreement-biased attention mechanism (Equation 4)
against a standard similarity-seeking attention baseline for inter-agent communication, so we
cannot yet attribute the communication ablation’s effect specifically to the disagreement-seeking
design choice, as opposed to inter-agent communication in any form.
8.5
Engineering and reporting limitations
• Several numerical values in earlier internal drafts of this paper (headline AUC, ablation baseline
settings, adapter parameter counts) did not fully agree with our raw logs; we have corrected what
we could reconcile for this draft and flagged the remainder (marked † throughout, and listed
exhaustively in Appendix F).
• We do not currently report a breakdown of how much of CIDA’s own forward-pass cost, versus
the frozen backbone’s, contributes to the latency figures in Table 6 (Section 3.7).
• Answer-extraction code for the language-reasoning baselines has not been audited specifically for
the very low base rates observed on some BIG-Bench Hard subtasks (Section 6.3).
• Efficiency numbers beyond Table 6 (specifically, some throughput and per-token cost figures
that appeared in an earlier internal draft) were dropped from this version because they conflicted
with our own source measurements and we could not reconcile the discrepancy before this draft’s
deadline; only the figures we could confirm are reported here.
9
Future Directions
We separate this section into near-term items directly motivated by the limitations above, and longer-
term, more speculative design directions that are part of the CIDA design space but have not been
evaluated in this paper in any form.
9.1
Near-term: closing the gaps in Section 8
• Equal-capacity controls. Train a LoRA adapter and a soft-prompt (prefix-tuning) baseline,
each with a parameter count matched to the CIDA adapter used in Table 5, on the identical
training data, and compare all three on the identical five-benchmark evaluation. This is the single
experiment most likely to change how the reasoning results in this paper should be interpreted,
and we consider it the top priority for follow-up work.
• Multi-seed imaging replication. Repeat the CheXpert training run (Section 6.1) across at least
3–5 seeds to obtain a variance estimate for the headline AUC and ECE, and repeat the component
ablation under the same multi-seed protocol to determine which of the five ablated components’
effect sizes are distinguishable from noise.
• Real per-agent instrumentation. Build logging infrastructure to record real per-round agent
beliefs, uncertainty terms, and attention weights on held-out examples, replacing the conceptual
schematic in Figure 3 with actual measured trajectories, and enabling a genuine test of whether
the four agents differentiate in a way that resembles their names.
• Answer-parser audit. Manually audit a sample of the automated answer-extraction failures
on the lowest-scoring BIG-Bench Hard subtasks to separate genuine reasoning failures from
answer-format mismatches (Section 6.3).
• External medical validation. Any path toward clinical relevance for the imaging component
would require, at minimum, evaluation on external datasets not used in training (e.g., ChestX-
ray14, PadChest) and, eventually, prospective evaluation with radiologist oversight; we have not
begun this process and flag it as a substantial, multi-stage undertaking rather than a near-term
item.
9.2
Design-space extensions (not evaluated in this paper)
The following ideas are part of the CIDA design space but are explicitly not evaluated anywhere in
this paper; we list them as hypotheses for future work, and are careful below to mark exactly which
24


=== PAGE 25 ===
parts are unmeasured.
• Latent Rollback. If inter-agent belief variance remains above a threshold after the full T rounds
— i.e., the agents have not converged — perturb the agent states and run additional rounds, rather
than forcing a consensus at a fixed, pre-specified T regardless of whether deliberation has actually
settled. This would make T adaptive per-input rather than fixed, at the cost of variable inference
latency.
• Latent Swarm. Compose several CIDA modules, each with its own set of agents, that exchange
compressed latent vectors with each other rather than with a single shared consensus step,
potentially reducing the communication volume relative to running several independent CIDA
modules with a naive all-to-all exchange. Any reduction in communication volume from this
design is currently unmeasured and speculative.
• Latent prompt storage. Store system prompts as learned embeddings, analogous to the plan
tokens of Section 3.6, to reduce the token cost of long, static system prompts in production
deployments. We note, as a caution for anyone pursuing this direction, that embeddings of this
kind can be partially inverted back to approximate text by an adversary with white-box or even
some black-box access to the model, so this technique should not be treated as a confidentiality
or security mechanism without dedicated study of its invertibility.
• Adaptive agent and round counts. Rather than fixing M=4 agents and a constant T across all
inputs, learn to allocate more agents or more rounds to inputs the model itself is uncertain about,
in the spirit of adaptive-computation approaches more broadly.
• RL-trained deliberation. Rather than training the CIDA module purely against the downstream
task loss, use a reinforcement-learning signal that directly rewards useful disagreement or efficient
convergence during deliberation, in the spirit of how reasoning-focused reinforcement learning
has shaped explicit chain-of-thought behavior [3].
• Multimodal CIDA. Extend the plug-in beyond a single frozen backbone per input to settings
where different agents receive projections derived from different modalities (e.g., image and text)
of the same underlying example, allowing the disagreement-seeking communication mechanism
to operate across, rather than only within, a single modality.
• Accuracy–latency Pareto analysis. Extend the single-operating-point efficiency profile in
Section 6.4 into a full Pareto curve, varying P and T against CoT, LoRA, and soft-prompt
baselines at matched compute budgets, to characterize the trade-off surface rather than a single
comparison point.
10
Broader Impact
Calibrated uncertainty estimates matter directly for any setting where a model’s output is meant to
support, rather than replace, a human decision-maker — clinical decision support being the most
immediate example touched by this paper’s imaging experiments. A well-calibrated model that says
“I am 60% confident” when it is right roughly 60% of the time is more useful, and arguably safer, to a
downstream human reviewer than an overconfident model whose stated probabilities do not track its
actual accuracy, even if the two models have identical raw discriminative accuracy. This motivates
why we report ECE alongside AUC throughout this paper, rather than AUC alone.
Separately, methods that reduce the compute cost of a given level of model capability — as CIDA’s
small added parameter count and reduced generation length aim to do relative to chain-of-thought
decoding — can lower the barrier to entry for research groups and institutions without access to large
GPU clusters. This paper is itself an example of the kind of work such methods might enable: every
experiment reported here was run on a single free-tier or Pro-tier cloud GPU instance, not a dedicated
research cluster, and we view the compute-constrained framing of this paper (Section 4.4) as a feature
relevant to broader impact, not only a limitation.
We reiterate, because it bears directly on how this paper could be misused if these caveats were
dropped: any clinical application of the imaging component described here would require prospective
validation, external dataset evaluation, and expert radiologist oversight that this paper does not provide,
25


=== PAGE 26 ===
and CIDA in its current form is a research prototype, not a validated diagnostic tool. Similarly, the
language-model reasoning results in this paper should not be read as evidence that CIDA-equipped
models are more reliable or trustworthy than their base models in any general sense; the regressions
on ARC-Challenge and HumanEval (Table 5) are a direct empirical counter-example to any such
blanket claim, and we would consider it a misuse of this paper’s results to cite only the GSM8K and
BIG-Bench Hard gains without the accompanying regressions.
11
Conclusion
We presented CIDA, a plug-in architecture that moves multi-agent deliberation from the token
space used by chain-of-thought and debate-style methods into the latent space of a frozen backbone,
structured around four separable components — perspective projection, contrastive communication,
reliability-weighted consensus, and latent planning — each individually ablatable and each, in this
paper, individually caveated. In feasibility experiments under heavily constrained compute, CIDA
improved a medical-imaging classifier’s AUC and calibration relative to reported baselines (while not
beating simple post-hoc temperature scaling on calibration alone), raised the multi-step reasoning
accuracy of a 7B-parameter language model relative to the same model without chain-of-thought
prompting (while regressing on knowledge-recall and code-generation benchmarks), and produced
substantially shorter generations than chain-of-thought decoding at a measured accuracy cost on the
one paired efficiency comparison we report. Every one of these results is preliminary in a specific,
stated way — single seed, small sample size, or missing capacity-matched control — and we have
tried throughout this paper to make the boundary between what we measured and what we merely
designed for as legible as possible, including through an explicit author checklist (Appendix F)
documenting corrections made to this draft. The central open question this paper leaves for future
work is not “does CIDA work” but the narrower and more answerable “how much of CIDA’s effect,
if any, survives a fair comparison against an equally-sized LoRA or soft-prompt adapter” — and we
release code, the trained language-model adapter, and this paper’s full derivations and reproducibility
material specifically so that other groups with more compute than we had available can run that
comparison.

Extended Qualitative Method Comparison

Author Checklist
This checklist was maintained during preparation of this paper and is reproduced here, essentially
verbatim, rather than silently resolved, in keeping with the emphasis throughout this paper on making
the boundary between measured and unmeasured claims explicit. Items are listed in the order they
were raised; several are referenced directly from the main text.
1. Headline AUC. An earlier draft used 0.939 in the abstract and headline table, while figure titles in
the source files instead read 0.9385, and the per-class values shown in the ROC legend (Figure 4)
average to approximately 0.912. These three numbers should, in principle, be the same quantity
31


=== PAGE 32 ===
computed three different ways from the same underlying predictions. We use 0.939† throughout
this draft as the best current estimate, mark it with a dagger everywhere it appears, and treat full
reconciliation against the raw prediction logs as an open item.
2. Ablation baseline. The ablation figure (Figure 6) shows a baseline AUC of 0.812, while Table 3
uses a reported literature baseline of 0.847 for plain DenseNet-121. We confirm here explicitly
that these are two different rows: 0.812 is our own frozen-DenseNet run under our specific
50k-subset protocol (used as the “no CIDA at all” reference point for the ablation), while 0.847 is
the externally reported figure used as a point of comparison in Table 3. An earlier draft’s ablation
figure caption stated a “50k validation set,” while an earlier draft of what is now Table 4 stated
“10k images, 5 epochs”; we confirm the setting used for every number in Table 4 and Figure 6 is
the same 50k-subset, 15-epoch protocol described in Section 5, and the earlier “10k, 5 epochs”
description was an error in that earlier draft, not a separate run.
3. Patient-level split. We have not yet confirmed, from our own data-splitting code, that the
CheXpert train/validation split used for this paper’s results is a patient-level split (i.e., that
no single patient’s images appear in both the training and validation partitions). This matters
because a study-level or image-level split that inadvertently leaks the same patient across train
and validation could inflate reported validation performance. We flag this as an unresolved, and
important, open item rather than assume the standard convention was followed correctly.
4. Reliability diagram. An earlier draft’s Figure 3 was captioned as a set of “reliability diagrams,”
but the actual image was a per-class accuracy/F1 bar chart (now Figure 5 in this draft, correctly
captioned). No true reliability diagram (predicted-confidence-vs-empirical-accuracy plot, binned
as in Equation 8) currently exists for this paper. Generating one from the logged predictions, with
10–15 confidence bins, is listed as a near-term item in Section 9 for anyone wishing to visually
inspect CIDA’s calibration beyond the scalar ECE number.
5. Adapter sizes. The 67M / 160M / 17M / <1M adapter parameter counts in Table 2 are copied
from earlier internal source tables; we have not independently re-derived each figure from the
corresponding model’s saved checkpoint size, and Appendix E marks all but the v3 figure as
unconfirmed pending that check.
6. Removed figures. An earlier draft’s Figure 6 (purporting to show per-agent belief trajectories
from real inputs) and Figure 7 (purporting to show per-agent attention maps) exhibited clear signs
of having been produced by an unconstrained generative image process rather than logged from
the model — a visible generator watermark, and misspelled role labels (“Skteptic,” “Integrax”)
that do not match this paper’s own terminology, along with duplicated panels. These must not be
presented as model output under any circumstances. The former Figure 7 has been removed from
this draft entirely; the former Figure 6 has been replaced by the explicitly labeled conceptual
schematic now shown as Figure 3, captioned throughout this draft as not measured output.
Producing real versions of either figure requires the per-agent logging infrastructure listed as a
near-term item in Section 9, which does not yet exist.
7. Answer extraction. The answer-parsing code used for the language-reasoning baselines has
not been audited, and the very low base (no-CoT) scores on several BIG-Bench Hard subtasks
(Section 6.3) may be partly attributable to parser failures rather than the base model’s inability to
solve the underlying problems. Flagged as a near-term audit item in Section 9.
8. Efficiency numbers. The efficiency numbers reported in Table 6 are drawn from our own source
measurement table. A small number of additional efficiency claims that appeared in an earlier
internal draft — specifically a 4.77× speedup figure, a −14% figure, a −72% figure, and a
per-token cost claim — were dropped from this version because they conflicted with the source
table we could verify, and we could not reconcile the discrepancy before finalizing this draft.
Only the four rows shown in Table 6, which we can trace directly to a verified measurement, are
reported here.
This checklist is, by design, incomplete in the sense that new items may surface as the near-term
action items in Section 9 are carried out; we intend to maintain and publish an updated version of this
checklist alongside any future revision of this paper or its accompanying code release.
